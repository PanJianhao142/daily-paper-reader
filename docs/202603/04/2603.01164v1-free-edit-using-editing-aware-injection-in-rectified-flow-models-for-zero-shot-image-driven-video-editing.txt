## Abstract 

Image-driven video editing aims to propagate edit contents from the modified first frame to the rest frames. The exist-ing methods usually invert the source video to noise using a pre-trained image-to-video (I2V) model and then guide the sampling process using the edited first frame. Gen-erally, a popular choice for maintaining motion and lay-out from the source video is intervening in the denois-ing process by injecting attention during reconstruction. However, such injection often leads to unsatisfactory re-sults, where excessive injection leads to conflicting seman-tics from the source video while insufficient injection brings limited source representation. Recognizing this, we pro-pose an Editing-awa RE (REE) injection method to mod-ulate injection intensity of each token. Specifically, we first compute the pixel difference between the source and edited first frame to form a corresponding editing mask. Next, we track the editing area throughout the entire video by using optical flow to warp the first-frame mask. Then, editing-aware feature injection intensity for each token is gener-ated accordingly, where injection is not conducted on edit-ing areas. Building upon REE injection, we further pro-pose a zero-shot image-driven video editing framework with recent-emerging rectified-Flow models, dubbed FREE-Edit. Without fine-tuning or training, our FREE-Edit demon-strates effectiveness in various image-driven video editing scenarios, showing its capability to produce higher-quality outputs compared with existing techniques. ## 1. Introduction 

In recent years, fruitful endeavours in diffusion models [10, 26] and rectified flow (RF) [17, 18] have been pursued, bringing a wide range of applications including text-to-image (T2I) [13, 24], text-to-video (T2V) [3, 8, 28, 32], and image-to-video (I2V) [2, 8, 32]. These models provide a pedestal for video editing, which requires that the gen-erations meet editing requirements while keeping the non-editing area unchanged. The current progress of video editing mainly lies in text-driven setting [7, 14, 22, 29]. However, to embrace more specific or localized editing, some approaches [6, 12, 19] propose image-driven video editing, which first performs image editing on the first frame with any image editing tool (e.g ., Photoshop) and then propagates the modification to the rest frames. This means the editing requirements can be specified using a reference image rather than text instruc-tions [15]. Technically speaking, the current image-driven 1

video editing methods follow the “inversion-then-editing” pipeline with an I2V model ( e.g ., SVD [2]). Here, the source video is inverted into noise, which would be de-noised later with the edited first frame as the conditional signal. Besides, since there is only limited motion represen-tation in such a pure “inversion-then-editing” pipeline, they leverage source-video representation additionally by inject-ing reconstruction internal representations ( e.g ., Query or Key) to the editing denoising process. Notwithstanding promising results, we argue that RF models have emerged as more powerful models than dif-fusion models on I2V, but the feasibility of using RF models for image-driven video editing is rarely explored. To make up for this gap, we first reproduce the afore-mentioned vanilla injection method with a pre-trained RF I2V model [8] under the “inversion-then-editing” pipeline, which replaces the model representation during editing with that during reconstruction. However, as seen in Fig. 1, it struggles to consistently maintain the appearance of the edited first frame. We argue that the unpleasing results of vanilla injection stem from its use of the same injection intensity for each token . Here, excessive feature injection would conflict with those modified areas in the first frame, leading to undesired content leakage. Conversely, insuffi-cient injection would bring limited source representations. Thus, vanilla injection is a sub-optimal solution. Given the above considerations, we propose an Editing-awa RE (REE) injection method, which modulates the in-jection intensity of each token according to whether it is edited or not. Specifically, we design a modulation weight 

λ to implement a linear interpolation with Query Q (or Key 

K) in the self-attention of the reconstruction branch and the corresponding ˜Q (or ˜K) of the editing branch. To com-pute the modulation weight λ, we first use differences of the source and edited first frame to mask the editing area. Next, we track the editing area throughout the entire video by evaluating optical flow [27] for the source video, which is used to warp the first-frame mask. Then, the modulation weight λ can be obtained by inversing the warped mask se-quence, where no injection is performed in the editing area to maintain appearance modification. We apply the proposed REE injection method into the pre-trained Flow model LTX-Video [8] to form a training-free image-driven video editing framework, which inspires the name of our method: FREE-Edit. Though simple, the extensive evaluation of FREE-Edit demonstrates our capa-bility of generating high-quality appearance- and tempo-rally consistent output. For example, as seen in Fig. 1, mo-tion cannot be preserved well in FREE-Edit w/o -injection due to the limited motion signal. Besides, FREE-Edit w/ 

vanilla feature injection always leads to conflicting seman-tics from the source video with the edited appearance. In contrast, the proposed REE injection yields the most visu-ally consistent results. To sum up, our contributions are: • We propose a training-free image-driven video editing framework FREE-Edit, which equips an Editing-awa RE 

(REE) feature injection to a pre-trained Flow model. • Our REE injection designs a modulation weight λ, which uses optical flow to track the edited mask of the first frame and does not perform injection in editing areas. • Extensive experiments demonstrate that our FREE-Edit yields more elegant editing results in various scenarios than the current state-of-the-art editing methods. 

## 2. Related Works 

Text-driven Video Generation and Editing. As for Text-to-Video (T2V), [1, 3] insert temporal layers a pretrained Text-to-Image (T2I) model [24] to obtain temporal mod-eling. Another line of methods [8, 32] employs 3D VAE to incorporate 3D convolutions, which operate videos both spatially and temporally. Besides, text-driven video editing expects to generate an edited video that adheres to the target prompt while the original motion and semantic layout are preserved. Many efforts [7, 14, 16, 22, 31] expand the U-Net architecture of a pre-trained T2I diffusion model ( e.g ., SD [24]) along the temporal axis for 3D correspondence, where the denoising process is performed under the target-prompt guidance. Recent progress [29] has also included RF models to perform editing. 

Image-to-Video Generation and Editing. Several rep-resentative works [2, 8, 32] establish the foundation for image-to-video (I2V). Then, a group of methods [8, 28] propose a first-frame conditioning model with rectified-flow training procedure. Besides, some techniques [6, 12, 19] use these I2V models to carry out image-driven video editing by propagating the first edit frame. Here, besides training-free AnyV2V [12] and VideoShop [6], I2VEdit [19] performes finetuning on temporal attention layers for coarse motion extraction, which however, is time-and resource-consuming. Moreover, very recent work Go-with-the-Flow [4] proposes a motion-controlled method by fine-tuning video diffusion models, which also allows for first-frame guided video editing. This paper follows the training-free trend and designs an 

Editing-awa RE (REE) feature injection method on a pre-trained flow I2V model. Specifically, we use optical flow to propagate the edited area in the first frame and then gen-erate the modulation weight λ to ensure those pixels in the editing area are not subjected to improper feature injection.

## 4. Method 

Given a source video X = [X0, X1..., Xrn ], image-driven video editing synthesizes the target video Y =[Y0, Y1, ..., Yrn ] that propagates the appearance of the first edit frame ˆX1 while remaining the rest parts unchanged (e.g ., motion). Here, since temporal compression ratio r, we have z = [ z0, z1..., zn].

4.2. The Proposed REE Injection and FREE-Edit 

Overview of our REE Injection. From Fig. 1, we can ob-serve that vanilla injection always shows undesired content leakage, since excessive injection would conflict with the semantics of those modified areas in the first frame. How-ever, insufficient injection often cannot bring enough mo-tion signals. In view of the above dilemma, this paper pro-poses an Editing-awa RE (REE) feature injection method, which only performs injection in the non-editing area, thus preserving the editing content. We notice that the vanilla injection uses the same injec-tion intensity for each pixel or token even though the editing and non-editing areas are modified to different degrees. To deal with this, we propose to adaptively modulate the injec-tion intensity of each token according to its editing degree. Formally, we define λ ∈ R(1+ n)×l×1 as such a modulation weight, which is shared across channels. Then, we can give a unified formulation of Query ( ¯Q) and Key ( ¯K) in our REE injection during the editing denoising process: 

¯Q = λQ + ( 1 − λ) ˜Q, ¯K = λK + ( 1 − λ) ˜K, (7) When λ = 0, feature injection is disabled. When λ = 1,our REE injection degenerates to the vanilla injection. 

How to Calculate Modulation weights. Then, with these masks, we calculate the modulation weight λ of all (1 + n) latent frames, where injection is only conducted in the non-editing area. Note that previous text-driven technique Fatezero [22] also explores attention blending with masks, which are gen-erated from cross-attention maps of the text token associ-ated with the foreground object. However, cross-attention maps struggle to indicate editing regions in certain editing types ( e.g ., adding objects), where the location of the added object is uncertain. In contrast, we use optical flow to track editing regions, which can be well adapted to various edit-ing types. Besides, our method differs from TokenFlow [7], which propagates diffusion features during editing based on inter-frame correspondences calculated during inversion. Specif-ically, TokenFlow performs correspondence propagation in text-driven editing, whereas FREE-Edit employs editing-mask propagation in image-driven editing.

5.4. Quantitative Results 

In Tab. 3, we further conduct a quantitative evaluation on the I2V-Edit-Benchmark. We also include results for FREE-Edit w/o injection and w/ vanilla injection. For whole-image measurement, our standard FREE-Edit achieves the highest CLIP score and lowest Warp Error , demonstrating its superiority in both edit consistency and temporal coher-ence. Besides, our method achieves the best results on all metrics for non-editing areas. Specifically, as a training-free method, our method has 19.8% higher PSNR than I2VEdit. Besides, we record the time consumption of each method over 10 editing trials with 41 frames per edit. Thanks to the real-time performance of LTX-Video-2B [8], our FREE-Edit achieves the fastest speed. The three variants of our FREE-Edit achieve roughly comparable speeds. Note that I2VEdit requires additional training for motion extraction, where it takes about 2 hours for a 41-frame editing video on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. We also conduct a user study by applying a standard two-alternative forced-choice format for each participant. Our REE injection can be equipped on different RF models. In this paper, we use LTX-Video-2B [8] as the default I2V model since its real-time generation. ## 7. Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose an Editing-awa RE (REE) feature injection method, which designs a modulation weight λ to adaptively assign feature injection weights for each token. Specifically, we first use optical flow to warp the automat-ically calculated first-frame editing mask, and thus obtain editing masks for subsequent frames. Based on them, we compute the modulation weight λ to encourage that no in-jection is performed in editing areas. Further, we propose FREE-Edit, which integrates our REE injection method with a pre-trained Flow model for image-driven video edit-ing. ## B. Discussion on Optical Flow Accuracy 

Ap-Fig. 3 presents complex examples with rotating cam-era views (lemon cake → strawberry cake) and fast mo-tion (turning head and returning). Our REE injection can successfully propagate semantics during large viewpoint changes, whereas vanilla injection results in an incorrect cake shape. Here we also provide the results of editing-mask propagation. It can be seen that inaccurate optical flow may appear at object edges. FREE-Edit alleviates its influence to a certain degree using the prior of the pretrained I2V model. We also provide a failure case under fast motion, where vanilla injection yields unsatisfactory background and ob-ject appearance. Although our REE injection can generate a background consistent with the edited first frame, it can-not fully correct object appearance due to inaccurate optical flow. We argue that a stronger optical flow model can im-prove the performance of our FREE-Edit. 2
